7  Running a Study

Note
  • Design an ethically sound study that fits a workplace.
  • Use Goal-Question-Metric to turn a vague worry into a measurable question.
  • Scope a study to what you can actually run.
  • Match a question to the method, and push for better evidence.

Meridian’s team has learned enough to be dangerous. They know the vendor’s studies have holes, they know how to watch five people, and they know how to design a real experiment. The last thing standing between them and an answer is everything that isn’t the research: ethics, politics, and the discipline of turning “is the tool worth it?” into something with a number on the end.

A 2014 survey of practitioners found they most wanted questions like “how do I improve team productivity?” answered with their own data from their own systems — not controlled experiments or interviews with strangers (Begel and Nagappan 2014). That’s the frame for this chapter: you already have most of what you need; the skill is using it well.

7.1 Ethics First

Research on people needs ethical care even when it’s “internal.” “It’s just internal data” is not a free pass — the fact that you already have access doesn’t make using it harmless. Informed consent means people know what’s collected and how it’s used and can withdraw without consequence — and “it’s in the employment contract” is not consent, because the alternative is leaving the job.

When monitoring developers, even with consent, store data locally or give participants control over what’s shared. Monitoring that erodes the psychological safety that predicts team performance defeats the study before it starts. And get legal and HR involved before you collect individual-level data; retroactive approval is harder than proactive review.

7.2 Start with Your UX Team

If your organization has a UX (user research) team, give this job to them. UX researchers already do rigorous qualitative work — interviews, think-aloud sessions, thematic analysis — and rigorous quantitative work — usability metrics and the statistics to interpret them. They are already trained to ask “does this actually help people do their work?”, so an AI assessment program is not a new project for them; it’s their existing job pointed at a new tool.

If you don’t have a UX team, the earlier chapters are the toolkit you would otherwise hand them: the cheap-and-fast lesson for formative think-aloud and RITE studies, and the study-design lesson for the statistics to interpret the results. If you do have a UX team but want to check or follow along with their work, the checklist in the reading-studies lesson is what you use to sanity-check their conclusions. Either way, get someone outside your organization to challenge whether your metric actually measures what you care about.

7.3 Scoping the Question with GQM

“Does AI make our developers more productive?” can’t be answered until you know what “productive” means, which developers, on which tasks, over what period. The Goal-Question-Metric approach forces the issue (Basili et al. 1994):

  • Goal — what object, what property, from whose viewpoint, in what context?
  • Questions — whose answers would tell you whether the goal was met.
  • Metrics — the specific, operationalized number for each question.

For Meridian: Goal — understand the AI assistant’s effect on delivery speed, from the engineering manager’s view, in the current sprint process. Question 1 — does time-to-merge change after adoption? Metric 1 — median hours from PR open to merge, per sprint. Question 2 — does defect rate change? Metric 2 — bugs filed within 14 days of a merge.

A scoped question looks like: “Did time-to-first-review decrease for PRs submitted after we deployed the tool, compared to the three months before?” One team, one metric, one comparison, one period. If that scoped question doesn’t answer what stakeholders actually need to know, you’ve learned something important before running the study.

7.4 Practical Designs You Can Actually Run

Most practitioners can’t run a randomized trial — no random assignment, no control group, limited time. The realistic alternatives:

  • Retrospective analysis — study what already happened with data you already have (commit logs, tickets, review comments).
  • Interrupted time series — treat a policy or tool change as a natural experiment, looking for a trend change at the change point.
  • Internal survey — a structured survey of your own team, with clear scope and honest limitations.
  • A/B testing — if you ship software, random assignment of users to conditions is often already in your infrastructure.

Be honest about which design you actually have, not which you wish you had. And invest no more than a quarter of your budget in the first run — the first study rarely answers the question you thought you were asking, and a negative result often tells you what the real question is.

7.5 Sharing Results Without Overselling

Every study has limitations; state them before stakeholders ask. Distinguish “we found no effect” from “our study wasn’t designed to detect an effect.” Put confidence intervals, effect sizes, and sample sizes in the report, not just the appendix. And ask, before you send anything, “what happens if this appears on Reddit?”

When you can’t publish, write a memo with methods, results, limitations, and conclusions — and share it with the team that was studied, not just the leadership that commissioned it.

7.6 Matching Methods to Questions

As a quick reference:

  • “Is there a difference?” → controlled experiment or quasi-experiment.
  • “How large is the difference?” → effect sizes and confidence intervals.
  • “Why does this happen?” → interviews and observation.
  • “What do practitioners think?” → surveys.
  • “What patterns exist in our codebase?” → mining repositories.
  • “What causes what?” → natural experiments, DiD, ITS — or randomization.

The hardest step is usually the first: translating a vague worry (“is AI bad for us?”) into a specific, answerable question.

7.7 What the Evidence Actually Says (and What’s Oversold)

Stepping back, the honest summary of AI-productivity research is short: AI tools can speed up specific, well-defined tasks for individual developers; effects on end-to-end delivery are far less studied and less consistent; and nearly all studies are short-term, on narrow or artificial tasks, with volunteers or students. The “55% faster” headline is a task-level result being sold as a workday-level fact.

So when an executive cites a statistic, ask three questions: who funded it, what task was studied, and was there a control group? And when it’s your turn to buy a tool, ask for evidence from a context like yours — not students on toy tasks — with effect sizes and confidence intervals, on outcomes you actually care about.

7.8 Signs of Maturity

A team that has stopped asking “does the tool work?” and started asking harder questions is on the right track. Watch for these three:

  1. “How am I going to be measured in my annual performance review?”
  2. “What do we keep when token costs go up 20×?”
  3. “How much should I spend on insurance, and for what?”

The first keeps the tool from becoming a surveillance device; the second keeps you from building your workflow on a cost you don’t control; the third acknowledges liability for generated code. A team asking these is treating AI as a business decision, not a fad.

None of these traps are unique to AI. The same problems — proxy metrics, no control group, selection bias, overclaiming — appear in studies of agile, TDD, and every other practice, and they’re as old as empirical software engineering itself. The fix is to apply what the human sciences already know — which, as above, is usually already sitting in your UX team.

7.9 Misconceptions

Internal data has no ethical constraints because the company already owns it.
Collecting data for operations doesn’t authorize using it for research. The intended use matters, and research use needs separate consent and harm review.
A/B testing is a reliable all-purpose tool for internal studies.
It works when random assignment is natural and outcomes are measured automatically. Most questions about developer productivity don’t fit.
Negative results aren’t worth reporting.
A well-run null result prevents acting on a false assumption. Suppressing it recreates the file-drawer problem inside your own organization.
The evidence on AI and developer productivity is settled.
The field is young, dominated by short-term narrow studies, heavily industry-funded, and almost entirely lacking independent replication.

7.10 Check Understanding

Why is “it’s in the employment contract” insufficient as a basis for consent?

Consent must be specific, informed, and genuinely voluntary. An employment contract grants broad data-collection rights as a condition of employment, so participants can’t meaningfully withdraw without leaving their job — a power imbalance that undermines voluntariness.

A manager asks you to study “which developers are least productive” using commit data. Name two ethical concerns and one fix for each.

Commit data has low construct validity and can be gamed — use outcome metrics (defect rate, feature delivery time) instead of activity metrics. And ranking “least productive” individuals risks surveillance and discipline — commit in advance that results are for process improvement, not performance management.

A colleague says “hundreds of studies show AI improves productivity — the evidence is overwhelming.” What’s the key concern?

Publication bias: positive results get published, null results don’t. A large count of positive studies may just mean the negative ones are in file drawers. A meta-analysis that corrects for publication bias would give a more reliable picture than a head count.

7.11 Exercises

7.11.1 Scope It Down (15 minutes, pairs)

Take one broad question — “does using an AI assistant improve code quality?”, “does test-driven development reduce bugs?”, “does remote work hurt collaboration?” — and write a scoped version answerable in three months with a typical team’s data. Specify the metric, the comparison, and the time period.

7.11.2 Write the Limitations (20 minutes, solo)

You ran an internal A/B test: 30 developers used an AI code-review tool for eight weeks, 30 didn’t, and time-to-merge fell 22% in the treatment group. Write the “Limitations” section — sample size and power, external validity, alternative explanations, and ethics.

7.11.3 The Question Behind the Question (15 minutes, pairs)

A manager says, “We’re spending $X per developer per year on this AI tool. Are we getting our money’s worth?” Restate it as three specific, answerable research questions. For each, name the method and one data source. Then say which question matters most and why.
