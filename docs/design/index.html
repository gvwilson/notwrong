6  Designing a Study

Note
  • Choose the right method — qualitative, controlled experiment, or observational — for a research question.
  • Read p-values and effect sizes without overstating them.
  • Estimate the sample size a study needs.
  • Recognize the four kinds of validity threat.

Jonas has been pushed past his comfort zone. “OK,” he says to Maya, “we can’t just watch five people and we can’t just read the vendor’s studies. So how do we actually find out whether this tool helps?” This chapter is the answer: the three families of method you can reach for, and the small amount of statistics you need to use them without fooling yourself.

The right method follows the question, not your preference. “Is there a difference?” calls for an experiment. “Why does this happen?” calls for interviews. “What patterns exist in our codebase?” calls for an observational study. No single method answers all questions, which is why serious programs mix them.

6.1 Qualitative Methods: Interviews and Surveys

Use qualitative methods when you don’t yet know what to measure, when the phenomenon is about meaning and context, or when you need to understand why rather than that.

Interviews come in three kinds. Structured uses a fixed script; unstructured follows the participant’s lead; semi-structured — the workhorse — has a guide but allows follow-up. Ask open questions (“tell me about a time the suggestion was wrong in a way that surprised you”), probe what they say (“you mentioned you don’t trust it for security — can you say more?”), and avoid leading questions (“don’t you find it faster?”) and hypotheticals (“would you use it if…”) — people are poor predictors of their own behavior.

Surveys trade depth for breadth. Likert scales (“strongly agree” to “strongly disagree”) are common but have known problems; use at least five points. Watch the sampling strategy: a convenience sample (whoever responds) is usually biased toward engaged users, and a low response rate can produce non-response bias larger than sampling error. Pilot with three to five people before distributing.

Thematic analysis is how you turn interview data into findings. Open coding tags segments with labels close to what people said — use gerund coding (actions, not nouns): “switching off suggestions after a bad experience,” not “negative attitude.” Axial coding groups codes into themes that make a claim (“developers trust AI for syntax but not for logic”), and you keep collecting until you reach saturation — new data stops producing new codes.

6.2 Controlled Experiments

A controlled experiment manipulates a variable and measures the effect while holding everything else constant. The treatment group gets the intervention; the control group doesn’t. Randomization — assigning people to conditions by chance — is what makes causal claims defensible, because it spreads unknown confounders evenly across the groups.

Two ideas carry the statistics. The null hypothesis (H₀) is “no effect.” A p-value is the probability of seeing data at least this extreme if H₀ were true. Crucially, it is not the probability the result is wrong, and p < 0.05 is a 1920s convention, not a law of nature. You never prove an effect exists; you either reject H₀ or fail to reject it — and failing to reject doesn’t mean “no effect,” it may just mean your study was underpowered.

Significance says whether an effect is likely real; effect size says how big it is. Cohen's d measures the difference between two means in standard-deviation units — 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large. A huge sample can find a “significant” effect that’s too small to matter, which is why effect size should always travel with the p-value.

Statistical power is the probability of detecting an effect that’s actually there. Most software-engineering experiments are dramatically underpowered (Kampenes et al. 2007) — a typical 20–50 participants can only reliably detect a large effect (d > 0.8). The required sample size for a two-sample t-test is roughly

\[ n = 2 (z_\alpha + z_\beta)^2 \sigma^2 / \delta^2 \]

where δ is the smallest difference you care about, σ is the population standard deviation, z_α = 1.96 (for α = 0.05), and z_β = 0.84 (for 80% power). Calculate this before running the study, not after.

Blocking handles a known nuisance variable (like experience) by grouping people into strata before randomizing, so the senior developers can’t all land in one group by chance.

6.3 Observational and Natural Experiments

An observational study measures the world as it is, without assigning anyone to anything. Mining software repositories (MSR) — extracting data from git history, issue trackers, and CI logs — is the most common form, and it’s full of traps: GitHub projects are not a representative sample, survivorship bias hides the projects that died, and confounders lurk everywhere.

The trade-off is fundamental. Controlled experiments maximize internal validity but are artificial; observational studies maximize realism but can’t rule out confounders. Quasi-experiments sit between: difference-in-differences compares the change in a treated group against the change in a control group (assuming the two would have changed similarly otherwise); interrupted time series looks for a trend change at the moment an intervention landed. A natural experiment exploits real-world variation that approximates random assignment — say, a tool rolled out to some offices first because of licensing, not because they asked for it.

Finally, correlation is not causation. Three rivals explain any correlation between A and B: A causes B, B causes A, or a third thing C causes both. Randomization is what lets you rule out the last two.

6.4 The Statistics You Actually Need

You don’t need a degree to decode a methods section. The essentials:

  • Descriptive — mean is sensitive to outliers; the median isn’t. Software data (task times, file sizes, defect counts) is usually right-skewed, so medians and interquartile ranges are usually the honest summary.
  • Type I vs Type II error — a Type I error is a false positive (rejecting H₀ when it’s true); a Type II error is a false negative (missing a real effect). Power = 1 − β.
  • Multiple comparisons — run 20 tests at α = 0.05 and you expect one false positive by chance; correct with Bonferroni or false discovery rate control.
  • Confidence intervals — a 95% CI is usually more informative than a p-value because it shows direction and magnitude.

Worked power example. You want to detect a 3-defect-per-release reduction, and the standard deviation across teams is 4 defects. Plug δ = 3, σ = 4 into the formula: n = 2(1.96 + 0.84)² × 16 / 9 ≈ 28 teams per condition — 56 total. If you can only recruit 10 teams per condition, you’ll likely miss a real effect. Detecting a smaller difference (δ = 1) would need about 251 teams per condition.

6.5 The Four Validity Threats

Every threat to a study’s credibility falls into one of four buckets (Campbell and Stanley 1963; Wohlin et al. 2000):

  1. Conclusion validity — was the analysis right and powerful enough? (underpowering, violated test assumptions, uncorrected multiple comparisons.)
  2. Internal validity — can you conclude the treatment caused the outcome? (confounders, selection bias, novelty, learning, and boredom effects.)
  3. Construct validity — does the measurement capture the concept?
  4. External validity — does it generalize beyond this sample and task?

Many software-engineering experiments have strong internal validity but weak external validity — a randomized trial on students doing a 90-minute toy task may be airtight and tell you almost nothing about your team.

6.6 Misconceptions

p < 0.05 means the result is probably true.
It means the data would be unusual if there were no effect. Whether the result reflects reality also depends on prior plausibility and whether the analysis was pre-specified.
A large sample turns correlation into causation.
Sample size increases precision, not causal warrant. A million-point observational study is still observational.
Not finding a significant effect means the treatment doesn’t work.
It means the data is consistent with no effect. An underpowered study misses real effects all the time.
Qualitative research is just asking people what they think.
Rigorous qualitative work involves systematic collection, structured coding, audit trails, and intercoder reliability.
GitHub data represents software development.
Open-source GitHub projects are a small, self-selected slice of the software actually built. Commercial and embedded code is largely invisible to MSR.

6.7 Check Understanding

A study reports p = 0.03. A colleague says “there’s a 3% chance this result is wrong.” What’s actually correct?

If the null were true, you’d see a result this extreme 3% of the time. The p-value is not the probability the result is a false positive — that also depends on the prior probability an effect exists, which the p-value doesn’t capture.

500 developers using AI commit 8% more often (p = 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.15). What should you conclude?

Statistically significant but practically tiny: d = 0.15 is below the “small” benchmark, and with 500 participants the study can detect effects too small to matter. Whether an 8% commit bump means anything depends on construct validity — commits are a poor productivity proxy.

Why can’t you conclude “no effect” from a non-significant result?

A non-significant result means the data is consistent with H₀, not that H₀ is true. The study may simply have been underpowered. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

What’s the difference between internal and external validity, and why do they trade off?

Internal validity is whether you can conclude the treatment caused the outcome; external validity is whether the finding generalizes. Randomized experiments maximize internal validity at the cost of artificiality, while observational studies are realistic but can’t rule out confounders. You rarely get both at once.

6.8 Exercises

6.8.1 Power Calculation (15 minutes, solo)

Use n = 2(z_α + z_β)²σ²/δ² with α = 0.05 (z = 1.96) and 80% power (z = 0.84). You want to detect a 3-defect-per-release reduction, σ = 5 defects. Compute the minimum teams per condition. Then say, in one sentence, whether your organization could actually run this study — and what that implies.

6.8.2 Method Match (15 minutes, pairs)

For each question, name the method you’d use and one sentence on why:

  • Why do some teams at Meridian ignore the AI tool while others use it daily?
  • Did the tool change time-to-merge across the whole org?
  • Does the tool’s generated code carry more security bugs than human code?
  • What actually slows developers down most in their daily work?

6.8.3 Build Your Own (20 minutes, pairs)

Design an experiment for one claim below. Specify treatment and control, how you’d assign participants, what you’d measure and how, a realistic sample size, and two threats to external validity.

  • Pair programming reduces defect rates.
  • AI-assisted code review is faster than code review without it.
  • Developers who write tests first produce better-designed code.
