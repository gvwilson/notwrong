5  Cheap and Fast

Note
  • Run a small, fast, informal study rather than no study at all.
  • Recruit participants without a budget.
  • Run a think-aloud session and use the RITE method to iterate.
  • Present informal findings to skeptics without overclaiming.

In the early 2000s, Dennis Wixon’s team at Microsoft was evaluating software with the standard usability protocol: run every participant through the full test, compile observations, write a report, and wait for the next release to fix anything. Then they noticed the obvious. The second participant got stuck in the same place as the first. The third got stuck there too. By the time all eight finished, six had hit the same confusing label and abandoned the task the same way — while the team sat in the observation room taking notes for the report they’d write later.

Their response was blunt: fix the label between sessions (Medlock et al. 2002). If the first participant surfaces a serious problem, address it before the second arrives. The remaining participants can then find different problems instead of re-enacting the first. This is the Rapid Iterative Testing and Evaluation (RITE) method, and it captures the argument of this chapter: the point of research is to learn things that improve decisions, and elaborate protocols can get in the way of that goal just as surely as doing nothing.

5.1 What “Enough” Actually Means

The question is not “have we done enough research?” but “do we know enough to proceed?” — Erika Hall’s framing: research reduces uncertainty enough to act wisely (Hall 2019). Most decisions about tools and processes are made with almost no evidence at all, so a small, informal study almost always beats a meeting.

The standard for “enough” depends on the decision. Deciding whether to run a larger study: five sessions may do. Deciding whether to mandate a tool across five hundred developers: it won’t. This is the difference between formative and summative evaluation:

  • Formative — studying something to improve it. Small samples, rapid iteration, qualitative focus. The question is “what’s wrong?”, not “how often is it wrong?”
  • Summative — assessing whether something works well enough to deploy. More participants, controlled conditions, quantitative metrics.

Most of what practitioners can do without institutional support is formative — and mislabeling formative evidence as summative is the fastest way to lose credibility.

5.2 Finding Participants Without a Budget

Guerrilla research uses whoever is available and willing — convenience sampling, with all the limitations that implies. That’s fine for formative work, where you’re hunting for problems rather than estimating proportions. Practical recruiting: post in team or community Slack channels, ask colleagues in adjacent teams, or reach out to a handful of actual users. Avoid people you manage or who manage you (power dynamics contaminate the data), colleagues who know your hypothesis (they’ll confirm it), and outliers unless they’re your target.

Aim for people who fit your target profile, not whoever responds first. Recruiting the wrong participants produces findings that are confidently wrong rather than tentatively right. Five participants is typically enough to surface most problems in a focused workflow (Nielsen and Landauer 1993) — but that guideline applies specifically to formative usability testing, not to claims about what “most developers” do.

5.3 Running a Think-Aloud Session

A think-aloud protocol asks participants to verbalize their thoughts as they work: “Please say out loud whatever you’re thinking while you do this.” It surfaces reasoning that’s otherwise invisible — what they expected to happen, why they chose an action, when they realized something had gone wrong.

Equipment required: a participant, a computer, and a way to take notes. A screen recorder is useful but optional; you rarely need a full transcript. What matters is what you note during or immediately after.

Task-based testing gives participants concrete, realistic goals rather than open-ended exploration. Open exploration (“just try the tool”) produces impressions; concrete tasks produce observable behavior. Give three to five tasks, realistic and neutral — not designed to make the tool look good or bad. Then watch for: hesitation, backtracking, stated expectations that turn out wrong, indirect routes, and moments of giving up or working around the tool.

During the session, do not help when they get stuck, react to their frustration, or explain what the tool was “supposed” to do. The point is to observe what happens without you, because you won’t be there when real users hit the same wall.

Note

Participants will look to you for reassurance. They’re not doing it right or wrong — they’re showing you what actually happens. “You’re doing great, keep going” is a reasonable response to almost anything.

5.4 The RITE Method

Standard usability testing runs everyone through the same protocol and analyzes afterward — right when you need valid estimates, wasteful when participants keep rediscovering the same problem. RITE fixes the problem between sessions (Medlock et al. 2002): run the first session, identify the most severe problem, fix it (or document it as a firm recommendation), then repeat until sessions stop surfacing new problems.

RITE works because formative evaluation is about finding things to fix, not estimating proportions. A session that reveals a new problem beats one that confirms a problem you already know. It is not right when you need to compare two tool versions (you’re changing one between sessions) or a generalizable estimate.

5.5 Analyzing What You Found

Take notes during or right after each session, not the next day — memory for specific behavioral moments degrades within hours. Affinity mapping is a simple analysis for small studies: write each observation on a sticky note, cluster related ones, and name each cluster with a claim (“developers hesitate before accepting suggestions they haven’t read”) rather than a label (“trust issues”).

Count how often each problem appeared, and report it honestly: “four of five participants had difficulty with X” is a finding; “80% of developers have difficulty with X” is an overgeneralization from the same data. Then write a two-page report, not a twenty-page one — lead with the decision, list findings by severity, include one representative observation per finding, and end with recommended actions.

5.6 Presenting Findings to Skeptics

Some colleagues will dismiss informal research as “not scientific.” They’re not entirely wrong, and conceding that quickly beats arguing the point. Lead with the decision, not the methods: “Before we spend three months on this integration, we watched five developers use a prototype for two afternoons. Here’s what we found.” Acknowledge limitations before someone else does, and present uncertainty as a reason to do more, not a reason to dismiss what you have.

The hardest skeptic is the one using methodological objections as cover for not wanting to find out anything that complicates a decision already made. That’s a political problem, not a research problem — better evidence won’t fix it.

5.7 What You Cannot Claim

Five participants can support claims about the existence of specific problems and patterns within the observed sample. It cannot support claims about proportions (“X% of developers have this problem”), causal effects (“the tool causes more bugs”), or comparisons (“tool A is 30% faster than tool B”). The limitation isn’t that small samples produce no information — it’s that they produce adequate evidence for some questions and inadequate evidence for others.

5.8 Misconceptions

Five participants is enough for any research question.
Five is enough to surface major problems in a focused formative study — not to make quantitative claims about a population.
Guerrilla research is just asking people what they think.
Systematic observation, structured tasks, consistent note-taking, and disciplined analysis are what separate research from anecdote.
If participants complete the task, there is no problem.
People often finish while confused, backtracking, and making wrong assumptions that happen not to matter for that one task.
You should tell participants what you’re studying so they can help.
Telling them your hypothesis primes them to confirm it. Give them tasks, not hypotheses.

5.9 Check Understanding

A team wants to compare two AI assistants with five participants and half a day. Should they use RITE? Why not?

No. RITE is for formative evaluation of a single product — you change the tool between sessions, which makes those sessions incomparable. For a comparison, each participant should use both tools on equivalent tasks in counterbalanced order, and you report what you observe without claiming statistical validity.

Three of five participants got confused at the same step. Someone wants to write “60% of developers will have this problem.” What’s wrong, and how to rewrite it?

A proportion from a sample of five carries a confidence interval so wide (roughly 15%–95%) that “60%” implies precision it can’t support — and “developers” implies generalizability a convenience sample doesn’t have. Honest rewrite: “In three of five sessions, participants had difficulty at this step.”

Why is think-aloud usually more informative than asking people afterward what they found difficult?

Post-task recall is unreliable: people forget confusion they recovered from, summarize impressions instead of specific events, and rationalize behavior. The think-aloud captures real-time reactions — including confusion that was later resolved and wrong assumptions that happened not to fail.

5.10 Exercises

5.10.1 Design a Think-Aloud Session (20 minutes, pairs)

Your team is considering switching code-review tools. Write a plan for a 45-minute think-aloud: three tasks you’d give, a recruitment message for five colleagues on another team, and a simple note-taking template for hesitations, errors, and completions. Then name one element of your design that might bias your observations and how you’d address it.

5.10.2 Run a Live Think-Aloud (30 minutes, pairs)

Pair up. One of you is the researcher, the other the participant. The participant takes a small real task — summarize a pull request, or write a short function — using the AI assistant you actually have access to, thinking aloud throughout. The researcher uses the note-taking template from the previous exercise and does not help. Swap roles. Then compare notes: what did each of you feel was hard, versus what did the other person observe as hard?

5.10.3 Respond to a Skeptic (15 minutes, solo)

A colleague says: “This is too informal to be useful. To study whether this tool helps, we need a controlled experiment with random assignment and statistical analysis — anything less is anecdote.” Write two paragraphs: one acknowledging the legitimate concern, one explaining when informal evidence is more useful than waiting for the resources to run a formal study.
