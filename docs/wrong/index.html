3  How Studies Go Wrong

Note
  • Recognize the recurring measurement traps in AI-productivity studies.
  • Match a vendor claim to the specific trap it’s falling into.
  • Diagnose why self-report, selection bias, and weak baselines mislead.

Meridian’s team spends an afternoon reading the vendor’s evidence pack. Priya walks them through a dozen charts, each one showing the tool in a flattering light. Maya, Dev, and Jonas each find themselves nodding at a different chart — for a different bad reason.

The traps below are the most common ways AI-productivity studies go wrong. At least one of them is almost certainly operating in your organization right now. Each has a one-line tell — a phrase or number that should make you reach for this chapter.

3.1 Timing Artificial Tasks

The famous “55% faster” result came from a single greenfield task: implement an HTTP server in JavaScript, from scratch, in ninety minutes (Peng et al. 2023). Real software development is navigating a codebase someone else wrote, interpreting an ambiguous ticket, coordinating with three colleagues, and sitting in four meetings. Speed on a toy task doesn’t predict speed on any of that.

Tell: the task is greenfield, time-boxed to under a day, and done by developers with no other obligations. Jonas is the character most likely to fall for this — toy tasks are exactly where the tool makes him feel fast.

3.2 Measuring Only the Easy Half

Code generation gets faster, and that half is easy to measure. The other half is harder: time spent reviewing AI output for correctness, time lost debugging confident-but-wrong suggestions, security holes in plausible-looking code, and technical debt from suggestions that solved the immediate problem while ignoring the design around it (Pearce et al. 2022). A 2025 evaluation of five major LLMs found that none produced web-application code meeting industry security standards (Dora et al. 2025). If AI raises code volume without raising review capacity, cycle time can worsen.

Tell: the study measures time-to-first-code, not time-to-merged, and never mentions review, defects, or rework. Dev’s dashboard — PRs merged — is this trap in spreadsheet form.

3.3 Before/After With No Control Group

You roll out the tool in January; by June, PRs ship faster. Also between January and June: you hired three developers, rewrote the CI pipeline, and switched cloud providers. Without a group that didn’t get the tool, you can’t separate the AI effect from everything else that changed.

Tell: there is no comparison team, or the “control” is the same team in the past. Dev’s “PRs up 18%” is a before/after with no counterfactual.

3.4 Asking Developers If They Feel More Productive

“87% of developers report feeling more productive with AI” gets cited as proof that the tool works (Liang et al. 2024). Three things make self-report systematically misleading: the novelty effect (new tools feel fast until the shine wears off), the Hawthorne effect (people perform differently when observed), and social desirability bias (people tell the survey what it wants to hear, especially when management picked the tool). Feeling faster isn’t being faster.

Tell: the headline number is “X% report feeling more productive,” not a measured outcome. Jonas is the poster child for this one.

3.5 Treating Adoption Rate as a Success Metric

“We’ve hit 90% adoption across engineering” is a procurement outcome, not a productivity outcome. Adoption measures whether the tool is installed and opened — not whether suggestions are useful, whether they’re accepted thoughtlessly, or whether the accepted ones are correct. Adoption is easier to measure than benefit, which is exactly why it gets reported instead.

Tell: the number is “adoption,” “seats,” or “activation,” not any outcome. Priya leads with this chart.

3.6 Comparing Volunteers to Non-Volunteers

A study that compares developers who chose to use AI against those who didn’t is comparing two different populations, not two conditions. Early adopters are more motivated to experiment, more comfortable with new tools, and more likely to already be high performers. Selection bias means any difference may be a property of the person, not the tool. This is the single most common flaw in industry AI-productivity reports — a two-year longitudinal study found Copilot users were already more active than non-users before the tool was introduced (Stray et al. 2026).

Tell: the groups self-selected; nobody was randomly assigned. If Dev had let developers “opt in” to the rollout, his numbers would carry this flaw.

3.7 Treating Suggestion Acceptance Rate as a Quality Signal

Assistants report what fraction of their suggestions developers accept, and vendors present a rising acceptance rate as a quality improvement. Acceptance measures whether the code looked plausible enough to press Tab — not whether it was correct, secure, or maintainable. Field reports from companies like ZoomInfo lean on acceptance-rate figures for exactly this reason (Bakal et al. 2025). Under time pressure, developers accept more suggestions, including insecure ones (Pearce et al. 2022).

Tell: the metric is “acceptance rate” or “suggestions shown.” A rising acceptance rate is often a signal that people stopped reading.

3.8 Comparing AI to Nothing

A study that compares AI-assisted developers to a control group using nothing has chosen a baseline that doesn’t exist in practice. Developers without an AI assistant still use Stack Overflow, docs, and their teammates. The relevant question is whether the AI tool beats those alternatives, not whether it beats sitting in silence.

Tell: the control group “used no assistance” rather than “used the tools developers already have.” Any tool looks good against nothing.

3.9 The Tells at a Glance

Paste this on the wall next to your dashboard:

  • Greenfield task under a day → artificial task
  • Time-to-first-code, no review/rework → easy half
  • No comparison team → no control group
  • “% report feeling faster” → self-report
  • “adoption / seats / activation” → procurement, not productivity
  • Groups self-selected → selection bias
  • “acceptance rate” → plausibility, not quality
  • Control used nothing → weak baseline

3.10 Check Understanding

Your company surveys developers three weeks after rolling out an AI assistant; 78% report feeling more productive. Dev says this proves it works. Name two specific flaws.

First, three weeks falls inside the novelty period, when enthusiasm inflates self-report regardless of real performance. Second, feeling productive is not being productive — self-report is distorted by the Hawthorne effect and social desirability bias, especially when management chose the tool.

A study compares fifty developers who volunteered to use an AI assistant against fifty who didn’t. The AI group closes 25% more tickets. What’s the likely alternative explanation, and what design change fixes it?

Selection bias: volunteers differ from non-volunteers in ways that predict productivity independent of the tool. Random assignment — giving the tool to a random half of developers — makes the groups comparable on average.

A vendor’s acceptance rate rose from 25% to 40% last quarter, which they call a “quality improvement.” What’s wrong with that reading?

Acceptance measures whether a suggestion looked plausible, not whether it was correct, secure, or maintainable. A rise can reflect better suggestions — or more time pressure, simpler tasks, or habituation. Without a separate quality measure, acceptance rate says nothing about whether the code got better.

“We’ll measure lines of code per developer per week, two weeks before and after adoption.” Identify at least two flaws.

Lines of code has low construct validity as a productivity measure, and a two-week window sits entirely inside the novelty period. A better design uses an outcome-linked metric (deployment frequency plus change failure rate, or cycle time) over at least three to six months, with a team that didn’t adopt the tool as a control.

3.11 Exercises

3.11.1 Spot the Trap (20 minutes, pairs)

For each vendor claim below, name the trap from this chapter it’s falling into, and write one sentence on what you’d need to see to actually evaluate it:

  1. “Teams using our assistant merged 23% more pull requests last quarter.”
  2. “92% of developers say they feel more productive with our tool.”
  3. “Our acceptance rate hit 41%, up from 27% a year ago.”
  4. “Adoption across your engineering org is now 88%.”
  5. “In our study, developers with the tool finished a coding task 30% faster than those without it.”

3.11.2 Audit Your Own Dashboard (15 minutes, solo)

List three metrics your organization currently uses (or has recently used) to judge a tool or process change. For each, name the trap from this chapter it falls into — or, if it avoids them all, say in one sentence why.

3.11.3 Redesign One Flawed Study (15 minutes, pairs)

A company plans to “survey developers about perceived productivity after two weeks, compare to a pre-adoption survey, and report the percentage who feel more productive.” Identify three problems, then in a few sentences describe a better design: what to measure, over what period, and how to build a comparison group.
