2  What “More Productive” Means

Note
  • Explain why “productivity” is a definition problem before it is a measurement problem.
  • Judge whether a metric actually captures the thing it claims to measure.
  • Recognize the classic proxy-metric traps and Goodhart’s Law.
  • Reproduce how the “10x programmer” claim collapses as the definition tightens.

Back at Meridian, Dev has a number for the CTO ready to go: pull requests merged per week. It went up 18% after the AI tool shipped, so the tool is working. Case closed.

Maya asks the one question that stops the room: “Working at what?”

Before you can measure whether AI makes programmers more productive, you have to decide what “productive” means — and that turns out to be the hard part. A 2021 Microsoft paper called SPACE argued that developer productivity has at least five dimensions — satisfaction, performance, activity, communication, and efficiency-and-flow — and that optimizing one (say, commit frequency) routinely degrades the others (Forsgren et al. 2021). If you only measure what is easy to count, you optimize for the wrong thing.

2.1 Construct Validity: Does the Number Mean What You Think It Means?

Construct validity is the degree to which a measurement actually captures the concept it is supposed to capture. A metric has high construct validity when changes in the metric reliably reflect changes in the thing you care about.

Productivity is unusually hard to operationalize. In manufacturing it means “widgets per hour” — output and time are both measurable. Software output is not homogeneous: a ten-line bugfix can be worth more than a thousand-line feature, or vice versa. Much of the work is invisible (reading code, reviewing, helping a colleague). The effects are delayed (a shortcut today becomes defects in six months). And most of a developer’s day isn’t even writing code: a field study tracking developers for two weeks found coding occupied roughly a quarter of the day, with meetings and coordination consuming the rest (Meyer et al. 2017).

The consequence: construct validity is usually assumed rather than demonstrated, especially in industry studies. Dev’s “PRs per week” is a perfect example — it’s a number, but it isn’t “productivity.”

2.2 Proxy Metrics and How They Go Wrong

A proxy metric is a measurable quantity you use as a stand-in for something harder to measure. The usual proxies for developer productivity, and the way each one gets gamed:

  • Lines of code — incentivizes verbosity; punishes refactoring and deletion. In 1982, Apple tracked LOC until Bill Atkinson deleted ~2,000 lines of QuickDraw in one week and management stopped asking (Sadowski and (eds.) 2019).
  • Commit frequency — incentivizes small, meaningless commits.
  • Story points completed — incentivizes inflating the estimates.
  • Pull requests merged — incentivizes trivial PRs and discourages hard work.
  • Bugs closed — incentivizes closing without fixing.

There’s a name for the general phenomenon. Goodhart's Law says that when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure (Goodhart 1984). The moment people know they’re being scored on commit count, they make more, smaller commits. The number improves; the work does not. The same trap bedevils AI-productivity studies, which is why the next chapter treats it as the first mistake to watch for.

Note

In the early 1980s, Apple measured programmer productivity by lines of code per day. Bill Atkinson, who wrote the QuickDraw graphics library, once submitted a timesheet showing -2000 lines: he’d replaced 2,000 lines with a faster, shorter algorithm. The metric penalized the improvement (Sadowski and (eds.) 2019).

2.3 Watch the “10x Programmer” Collapse

The best way to see that “productivity” is a definition, not a fact, is to do the arithmetic yourself. In 2000 Lutz Prechelt measured a mix of students and professionals, using seven different languages, as they each solved the same small algorithmic problem (Prechelt 2000). The raw data is in data/prechelt.csv — one row per person, with the language (lang), work time in hours (whours), and several quality metrics.

Start with the crudest possible claim: the fastest programmer is N times faster than the slowest. Compute the minimum and maximum work time and take the ratio. You’ll get something like 105x. Is there really a 105x programmer? Almost certainly not — that ratio compares the single fastest person to the single slowest, and the slowest “came back every day for a week.”

Now tighten the definition the way a careful researcher would:

  1. Homogeneous language. The mix of seven languages confounds the comparison (some languages suit the task better). Filter to just the Java rows. The ratio drops to about 17x.
  2. Compare the top 10% to the ordinary, not the worst. “Faster than normal programmers” should mean “faster than the slower half.” Compare the median of the top 10% (the 5th percentile) to the median of the bottom half (the 75th percentile). The ratio drops again — to around 11x, and under some reasonable definitions to 5x.
  3. Factor in quality. “Productivity” isn’t just speed — it’s value per effort. The z1000rel column is a reliability score (100 is perfect, lower means more defects), and z1000mem is memory use. Once you fold in “did it actually work?”, the picture changes again.

The lesson isn’t “there are no fast programmers.” It’s that the answer depends on the question, and a number that moves by a factor of 20 depending on how you ask is not a fact about the world — it’s a fact about your definition.

2.4 The Grown-Up Version: SPACE and DORA

If single metrics are hopeless, what do serious teams use?

SPACE (Satisfaction-and-well-being, Performance, Activity, Communication, Efficiency-and-flow) is a framework for remembering that productivity has multiple dimensions and that they trade off (Forsgren et al. 2021). DORA — the DevOps Research and Assessment program — found four metrics that correlate with organizational delivery performance: deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, and time to restore service (Forsgren et al. 2018). DORA metrics have better construct validity than raw activity counts because they tie to customer outcomes rather than to keystrokes.

Both are still imperfect. A team can score beautifully on DORA while building the wrong product, and neither framework will tell you whether the AI tool — specifically — is helping. What they give you is the right question to ask: which dimension are we trying to move, and how would we know?

2.5 Misconceptions

Writing more code means being more productive.
Wrong in both directions: deleting a thousand lines of tangled code and replacing them with a hundred clear ones improves the codebase, not harms it.
If a metric improves, the thing it measures must be improving.
Goodhart’s Law kicks in the moment people know they’re being measured. Better-looking numbers are often just behavior change around the measurement.
Productivity is an objective property you can measure with the right data.
Every operationalization embeds a choice about what matters — speed, quality, satisfaction, business value. Those are values choices, not measurement problems.
DORA metrics measure individual developer productivity.
They measure team and organizational delivery. Using them to grade an individual conflates levels of analysis and ignores the system factors that dominate individual variation.
Better measurement tools produce better management.
Measurement is not management. Even complete data needs judgment to act on, and improving productivity requires behavior change, not more dashboards (Sadowski and (eds.) 2019).

2.6 Check Understanding

What is construct validity, and why does it matter for a productivity metric?

Construct validity is how well a measurement captures the concept it’s meant to represent. It matters because a low-validity metric will mislead you: you can improve the number without improving the thing you care about. “Commits per day” has low construct validity — developers can raise it while becoming less productive.

Dev says “PRs merged per week went up 18%, so the tool is working.” Give two specific threats to that conclusion.

First, PRs-per-week is a low-validity proxy: developers respond to being measured by splitting work into smaller PRs without delivering more value. Second, it’s a before-and-after comparison with no control group, so any concurrent change (team growth, a new CI, a backlog cleanup) could explain the rise.

In the Prechelt data, why does the “fastest vs slowest” ratio (about 105x) overstate real differences between programmers?

Because it compares the single fastest person to the single slowest, and the slowest person is an outlier who took “every day for a week.” Tightening the definition — restricting to one language, comparing the top 10% to the typical programmer rather than the worst, and folding in quality — collapses the ratio to roughly 5x–17x. The headline number is an artifact of the definition.

2.7 Exercises

2.7.1 The Prechelt Data Lab (25 minutes, solo or pairs)

Load data/prechelt.csv into a spreadsheet or a short script. Compute, and write down each number:

  1. The min and max whours, and their ratio.
  2. The same ratio restricted to lang == "Java".
  3. The 5th and 75th percentiles of whours across all rows, and their ratio.
  4. The same percentiles restricted to Java, and their ratio.

Then answer in one sentence: which of these is the “right” answer to “how much faster is a fast programmer than a normal one” — and why does it depend on your definition?

2.7.2 Operationalize It (15 minutes, pairs)

Pick one of — code quality, developer satisfaction, onboarding effectiveness — and write a specific, measurable definition for it. Then write one paragraph on how your definition could be gamed (i.e., improved without the underlying thing improving). Trade with a partner and try to game their definition.

2.7.3 Goodhart in the Wild (10 minutes, solo)

Think of a metric you’ve seen used to judge developer performance. Describe one concrete way someone could improve the number without improving the work. If you can’t think of one from your own job, use “number of tickets closed per sprint.”
