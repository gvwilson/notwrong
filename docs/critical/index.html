4  Reading a Study

Note
  • Apply a structured checklist to a study in a few minutes.
  • Recognize HARKing, p-hacking, and publication bias in published work.
  • Spot conflicts of interest and flexible operationalization.
  • Produce a one-slide verdict on whether a claim is worth acting on.

Maya is the one on Meridian’s team who actually reads the papers the vendor cites. Her secret is that she doesn’t read them front to back — she reads them out of order, hunting for the weakest link. This chapter is that skill, compressed into a checklist you can run in about ten minutes.

The stakes are higher than they look. A 2023 McKinsey report proposed dozens of metrics for measuring individual developer productivity and was widely cited by managers as justification for tracking people (Elsayed et al. 2023). Kent Beck — one of the Agile Manifesto’s authors — wrote a pointed reply: the report treated productivity as an objective fact, when it’s actually a judgment made by people with interests (Beck 2023). The report wasn’t fraudulent. It was confident, plausible, and wrong in ways non-specialists couldn’t easily detect. That’s the failure mode this chapter teaches you to catch.

4.1 The Checklist

Run these five questions over any study, in this order. The answer to each is rarely “yes” or “no” — it’s usually “the paper doesn’t say.”

  1. Conclusion validity — Was the sample large enough to detect an effect of the expected size? Are effect sizes reported next to p-values? Were multiple comparisons corrected for?
  2. Internal validity — Is there a control group or credible counterfactual? Were participants randomly assigned? Could novelty, learning, or boredom explain the result? Are confounders addressed?
  3. Construct validity — Is “productivity” (or whatever the headline claims) explicitly and defensibly operationalized? Does the measurement capture what the claim is about?
  4. External validity — Who were the subjects, and do they resemble the population the conclusion addresses? How close is the task to real work? Is the conclusion proportionate to the evidence?
  5. Reporting — Are limitations discussed honestly? Is there a conflict-of-interest disclosure? Has the study been pre-registered or replicated?

4.2 How to Read a Paper (Out of Order)

Skip the introduction — it’s usually the last thing written and the most polished. Read instead: abstract → methods → limitations → results → back to the abstract. The methods tell you what the study could establish; the limitations tell you what the authors admit it couldn’t; the results tell you whether the abstract oversold it.

4.3 The Three Ways Papers Fool You

Three habits inflate the published literature, and you should assume at least one is present in any given paper:

  • HARKing — Hypothesizing After Results are Known. The researcher finds an interesting pattern, then writes the paper as if that pattern was predicted in advance. Tell: vague hypotheses in the intro that become suspiciously specific in the results, no pre-registration.
  • p-hacking — trying many analyses until one crosses p < 0.05, then reporting only that one. Tell: many conditions with exactly one “significant,” results that depend on dropping a few observations, no correction for multiple comparisons.
  • Publication bias — journals publish positive results far more often than null ones, so the literature overstates effect sizes. Tell: a field where you’ve never seen a null result.

Pre-registration — committing hypotheses and the analysis plan before data collection — makes the first two visible, which is why its absence is a yellow flag.

4.4 Who Paid for This?

Industry-funded studies in software engineering routinely produce results favorable to their funders — not through fraud, but through design choices, outcome selection, and framing. The Copilot study (Peng et al. 2023) was conducted by GitHub employees and circulated on arXiv before peer review. Ask three questions of any study: Who benefits if this is true? Who paid for it? Who employs the authors? A missing conflict-of-interest statement is itself a finding.

A subtler form of the same problem is flexible operationalization: choosing how to define “productivity” after seeing the data. Prechelt’s dataset produced ratios of 105x, 17x, 11x, or 5x depending on which definition was used — a number you can tune to tell any story you like (Prechelt 2000). A post-hoc operationalization is functionally p-hacking in a costume.

4.5 A Worked Example: The 55% Study

Run the checklist over the Copilot study Meridian keeps citing (Peng et al. 2023):

  • Conclusion validity — 95 participants on a single task; effect sizes are reported but the design is underpowered to say much about real work.
  • Internal validity — it was randomized (a strength), but the treatment and control conditions differ in more than the tool (novelty, task setup).
  • Construct validity — “productivity” is operationalized as time-to-complete on one greenfield task; that’s a thin slice of developer work.
  • External validity — a ninety-minute HTTP-server task by recruited developers doesn’t generalize to a six-month project on an existing codebase.
  • Reporting — funded and conducted by GitHub; no independent replication.

Verdict: the study is a real randomized experiment, but its headline number describes a toy task under vendor-friendly conditions — not your team. That one-slide sentence is what “reading a study” is for.

4.6 Misconceptions

Peer review detects p-hacking and HARKing.
Reviewers see the final manuscript, not the analysis history. Without pre-registration or raw data, they can’t tell a clean analysis from one tidied up after the fact.
Pre-registration guarantees a study’s results are valid.
It makes one class of problem visible — undisclosed analysis flexibility — but doesn’t fix flawed designs, bad measurements, or unrepresentative samples.
Industry-funded research is always biased toward favorable results.
“Always” is too strong. The concern is systematic, not universal: on average, funding source predicts outcome in ways independent replication often doesn’t support.
A well-cited paper is probably right.
Papers get cited because they tell a useful story, whether or not it holds up. Many highly-cited results in software engineering have failed to replicate.

4.7 Check Understanding

What’s the difference between p-hacking and HARKing? Can a study do both?

p-hacking is running many analyses and reporting only the one that reaches p < 0.05. HARKing is presenting exploratory findings as if they’d been predicted in advance. A study can do both: explore the data until something is significant, then write the paper as if that was the hypothesis all along.

A paper runs 12 subgroup comparisons and reports one significant at p = 0.04. Should you believe there’s a real effect?

No. With 12 independent tests at α = 0.05, you expect about 0.6 false positives by chance, so one barely-significant result is entirely consistent with noise. The honest response is a multiple-comparisons correction and treating the result as preliminary until replicated.

What is the file-drawer problem, and how does it distort what you read?

Null results tend to go unpublished, so the literature overrepresents positive findings. Reading all the published studies on a topic makes effects look more reliable than they are, because the null studies are sitting in file drawers.

A paper’s RCT shows developers “are more productive” with a tool, and the authors recommend every team adopt it. What’s the problem?

An RCT establishes causation in the studied sample under the studied conditions — usually “faster at one specific task.” Recommending “every team” is an external-validity claim the study can’t support, given its narrow sample, task, and context.

4.8 Exercises

4.8.1 Run the Checklist (25 minutes, pairs)

Take Peng2023 (Peng et al. 2023) or McKinsey2023 (Elsayed et al. 2023), and fill in the five checklist questions above — one sentence per question, each with a specific piece of evidence from the paper. End with a one-slide verdict: “this paper shows X, under conditions Y, and therefore / therefore doesn’t justify Z.”

4.8.2 Find the Spin (15 minutes, solo)

Find any recent paper or industry report on AI and developer productivity. Quote one sentence where the conclusion goes beyond what the methods support (the “spin”), and write one sentence describing what the evidence actually shows.

4.8.3 Funnel-Plot Reading (15 minutes, solo)

Find a meta-analysis of a software-engineering practice (code review, pair programming, TDD) that includes a funnel plot. In two sentences: what does the plot’s shape suggest about publication bias, and how does that change your trust in the reported effect size?
