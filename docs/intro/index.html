1  Is AI Worth It?

Note
  • Restate a vague worry about AI tools (“is this helping us?”) as an answerable research question.
  • Distinguish the kinds of question that call for qualitative versus quantitative methods.
  • Recognize why most coverage of AI-productivity studies can’t be trusted on its own.

In early 2023 a team at GitHub published a paper claiming that developers using Copilot completed a programming task 55% faster than those who did not (Peng et al. 2023). Within weeks that number was everywhere: all-hands decks, vendor sites, blog posts calling it a “landmark study.” Executives repeated it to justify buying tools for thousands of developers. Almost nobody asked the question that actually mattered: is 55% even a plausible answer to the question I have?

It turns out the study used 95 people doing one artificial task — building an HTTP server in JavaScript from scratch — for ninety minutes, and it was funded by GitHub, the company that sells Copilot. None of that makes the result wrong. It does mean “55% faster” describes a very narrow slice of what your team does all day, and it may not transfer to your question at all.

This workshop is about learning to answer the question your manager is going to ask you in two weeks:

We’re paying $X per developer per year for this AI tool. Is it worth it?

That question is smaller than it looks, because every word in it is doing hidden work. Worth what? To whom? Compared to what alternative? Over what time period? Most of this workshop is about turning those hidden words into things you can actually measure.

1.1 The Running Case

To keep the ideas concrete, every chapter will return to the same situation.

Meridian is a mid-sized company that builds software for supply-chain logistics. Three months ago it rolled out an AI coding assistant to all of its ~200 developers. The CTO now wants to know whether the subscription is paying for itself. Four people have been asked to figure it out:

  • Maya, a staff engineer — skeptical, wants evidence she can defend, and deeply suspicious of vendor numbers.
  • Dev, the engineering manager who chose the tool — wants a clean dashboard metric he can show the CTO, ideally one that goes up and to the right.
  • Jonas, a junior developer — the tool’s biggest fan, who genuinely feels faster with it, but has the least experience to judge whether that feeling is right.
  • Priya, a solutions engineer from the vendor — friendly, knowledgeable, and paid to keep Meridian as a customer.

Each chapter will hand this group a different way of attacking the question. Watch for the moments where Maya, Dev, Jonas, and Priya each feel sure of something — and where the methods in this book show one of them is wrong.

1.2 Two Ways to Answer the Question

When you set out to evaluate a tool, you can do it one of two ways, and the question — not your preference — decides which is right.

Quantitative methods measure things: how long a task takes, how many defects ship, how many pull requests merge in a week. They answer “how much” and “is there a difference.” They only work when you can define and count the thing you care about — which, as the next chapter shows, is harder for “productivity” than most people admit.

Qualitative methods describe things: what developers think of the tool, why a team adopted it and another didn’t, where people get stuck. They answer “why” and “what’s actually happening.” They only work when you can get close enough to real people to see their actual experience, not their survey answers.

Neither is more rigorous than the other. A good program mixes both: qualitative work often finds the right question, and quantitative work answers it. The trap is methodological tribalism — “we only do numbers” or “numbers can’t capture developer experience” — which usually means the speaker would rather not find out something that complicates a decision they’ve already made.

1.3 Why You Can’t Trust the Coverage

Most of what you’ll read about AI and developer productivity is filtered through several layers, each of which degrades accuracy:

  • Journalists read abstracts. Abstracts rarely report limitations, and the story “AI makes developers 55% faster” is a lot more shareable than “one study on one toy task found a speed-up.”
  • Press releases are marketing. They exist to generate coverage, not accuracy, and they’re written by people whose job is to make the funder look good.
  • Preprints look just like papers. To a non-specialist, an unreviewed arXiv preprint and a peer-reviewed journal article are indistinguishable — and a large fraction of AI-productivity studies are one, not the other.
  • Conflict of interest is the norm. A large share of these studies are funded by the companies selling the tools, so the default assumption should be that the deck is stacked, not that it isn’t.

The lesson isn’t “don’t believe anything.” It’s “trace the claim to the evidence, and ask what the evidence can actually support.” That’s the skill this workshop teaches.

1.4 Misconceptions

Peer review means a study is correct.
Peer review filters out obvious errors and implausible claims; it does not certify validity. Reviewers rarely re-run analyses or check the raw data.
A larger sample always makes a study better.
Sample size can’t fix a flawed design. Ten thousand people doing an irrelevant task tell you nothing about the question you actually care about.
Qualitative methods are less rigorous than quantitative methods.
They use different standards of rigor, not lower ones.
One well-designed study settles a question.
A single study reports what happened in one sample under one set of conditions. Findings become trustworthy through replication across different samples, contexts, and teams.

1.5 Check Understanding

What is the difference between a claim and evidence?

A claim is an assertion, such as “AI tools make programmers more productive.” Evidence is what a systematic study produces when it tests that claim, and it varies in quality depending on design, sample, context, and bias. A single study produces evidence for or against a specific claim under specific conditions; it does not prove or disprove the claim in general.

The GitHub Copilot study found a 55% speed increase. Dev says this means AI doubles productivity. Name two specific problems with that interpretation.

First, the study used a single artificial task, which doesn’t represent the range of work developers actually do. Second, “55% faster on one task” is not “doubles overall productivity” — productivity includes far more than task completion time, and the effect on the whole working day is unknown. Third, the study was funded by GitHub, which has a financial interest in Copilot looking effective.

Maya wants to use numbers only; Jonas says developer experience can’t be captured numerically. Who’s right?

Neither. The method follows the question. “How much time does this save?” is a quantitative question; “why do people hesitate to accept suggestions?” is a qualitative one. Dismissing either approach before knowing the question is methodological tribalism, not reasoning.

1.6 Exercises

1.6.1 Restate the Question (10 minutes, solo)

Meridian’s CTO asks, “Are we getting our money’s worth from this AI tool?” Write down three specific, answerable research questions that unpack that vague one — each with the metric you’d need, the comparison you’d make, and the time period you’d look at. Example: “Has median time-to-first-review for pull requests changed since we deployed the tool, compared to the three months before?” Keep them to one sentence each.

1.6.2 Qualitative or Quantitative? (10 minutes, pairs)

For each question below, say whether you’d primarily use qualitative or quantitative methods, and in one sentence why:

  • Do developers who use the assistant produce fewer defects?
  • Why do some teams on Meridian’s codebase use the tool daily while others ignore it?
  • How do developers decide when to trust an AI suggestion versus working it out themselves?
  • Does code review take less time when reviewers use the assistant?

1.6.3 Spot the Gap (15 minutes, pairs)

Pick one AI-productivity claim you’ve actually heard (for example, “AI cuts onboarding time in half” or “AI-generated code has more security bugs”). Find the study behind it — or the closest thing to one — and list, in one line each: who funded it, what the sample actually was, what task was studied, and what the headline claims versus what the methods actually show. Compare your list with your partner’s; the point is to feel how much a headline hides.
